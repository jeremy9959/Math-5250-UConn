    Applications of the QR, Singular Value, and Polar Decompositions

    Recommendations for illustrative examples in notes/Week7-8/W07.3.qmd (“QR, SVD, and polar decomposition”), chosen to connect with material already in the notes. Each entry gives the mathematical point, the connection to existing notes, and a suggested Python demonstration (NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib only, unless stated).

    Relevant context from the repository:

    • W05.1 already contains Gram–Schmidt (stated with the triangular change-of-basis matrix), orthogonal projection, closest points, and ordinary least squares solved via the normal equations X^{\top}XM = X^{\top}Y, with the projection matrix X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top}.
    • W05.2 contains adjoints, the spectral theorem, unitary matrices, orthogonal projections, and positive operators.
    • W04.3 contains the Jordan form and matrix exponentials; W02.3 contains determinants.
    • Norms (Week 9) come after this material, so norm-based statements (Eckart–Young, condition numbers) can be stated here and proved or revisited in W09.2.
    • No existing .qmd file uses executable {python} cells yet, so the first such example will establish the convention.

    QR decomposition

    QR 1. Least squares without the normal equations

    Mathematics. If X = QR with Q having orthonormal columns and R upper triangular and invertible, then the normal equations reduce to RM = Q^{\top}Y, solved by back substitution. The projection onto the column space becomes P_W = QQ^{\top}, which replaces X(X^{\top}X)^{-1}X^{\top} from W05.1. The column-by-column form of QR is exactly the triangular change-of-basis statement in the W05.1 Gram–Schmidt proposition, so R can be identified with the inverse of the matrix (a_{ij}) appearing there.

    Point to make. Forming X^{\top}X squares the condition number: \kappa(X^{\top}X) = \kappa(X)^2. This can be stated now and justified with the SVD later in the same lecture.

    Python. Fit a polynomial of degree 12–15 to samples on [0,1] using a Vandermonde matrix. Compare the coefficient error from np.linalg.solve(X.T @ X, X.T @ y) against np.linalg.qr followed by scipy.linalg.solve_triangular. With exact data generated from known coefficients, the normal-equations solution loses roughly twice as many digits.

    QR 2. The QR algorithm for eigenvalues

    Mathematics. Set A_0 = A, factor A_k = Q_kR_k, and set A_{k+1} = R_kQ_k = Q_k^{\top}A_kQ_k. Each A_k is orthogonally similar to A. Under mild hypotheses (e.g. eigenvalues of distinct absolute value) A_k converges to an upper triangular matrix, which gives a constructive view of Schur triangularization; for symmetric A the limit is diagonal, and the accumulated product Q_0Q_1\cdots Q_k converges to an orthonormal eigenbasis, which is the spectral theorem of W05.2 realized as an algorithm.

    Python. Run 50–100 unshifted iterations on a random symmetric 5\times 5 matrix and display A_k at several stages, or plot the size of the strictly lower triangular part against k on a log scale to show linear convergence at rate governed by ratios |\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|. Compare the diagonal with np.linalg.eigvalsh.

    QR 3. Volume and the Gram determinant

    Mathematics. For an n\times k matrix X of rank k with X = QR, the k-dimensional volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the columns is \prod_i |r_{ii}|, and this equals \sqrt{\det(X^{\top}X)}. Each r_{ii} is the distance from the i-th column to the span of the earlier ones (the closest-point result of W05.1), so the formula is “base times height” iterated. For square X it gives |\det X| = \prod |r_{ii}|, connecting to W02.3. Hadamard’s inequality |\det X| \le \prod \|x_i\| follows immediately.

    Python. Minimal; a short numerical check suffices. This example is primarily a proof-level illustration.

    QR 4. Orthogonal polynomials

    Mathematics. Apply Gram–Schmidt to 1, x, x^2, \ldots in C[-1,1] with \langle f,g\rangle = \int_{-1}^{1} fg\,dx; the result is the normalized Legendre polynomials. Other weights give Chebyshev, Hermite, and Laguerre polynomials. The discrete analogue is the QR factorization of a Vandermonde matrix at sample points, whose Q columns are values of polynomials orthogonal with respect to the discrete inner product. This illustrates QR in an abstract inner product space, consistent with the general J notation of W05.1.

    Python. Use numpy.polynomial to carry out Gram–Schmidt symbolically in the monomial basis and compare with numpy.polynomial.legendre; plot the first five.

    QR 5 (optional). Loss of orthogonality in classical Gram–Schmidt

    Mathematics. Classical Gram–Schmidt, modified Gram–Schmidt, and Householder reflections produce the same Q in exact arithmetic, but behave differently in floating point. Householder reflections H = I - 2vv^{\top}/v^{\top}v are also a natural example of orthogonal, self-adjoint operators from W05.2.

    Python. For a Hilbert or Vandermonde matrix, compute \|Q^{\top}Q - I\| for the three methods. Classical Gram–Schmidt typically loses orthogonality completely for n\approx 10–12.

    Singular value decomposition

    SVD 1. Geometry: the image of the unit sphere, operator norm, and condition number

    Mathematics. A = U\Sigma V^{\top} maps the unit sphere to an ellipsoid with semi-axes \sigma_iu_i, attained at v_i. Hence \max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\| = \sigma_1, \min_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\| = \sigma_n for square A, and the condition number is \sigma_1/\sigma_n. The v_i and \sigma_i^2 are the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the positive operator A^{\top}A (W05.2), which is how the existence proof proceeds. This leads directly into the operator-norm formula of W09.2, and justifies \kappa(X^{\top}X) = \kappa(X)^2 from QR 1.

    Python. For a 2\times 2 matrix, plot the unit circle with v_1, v_2 and its image with \sigma_1u_1, \sigma_2u_2, side by side.

    SVD 2. Low-rank approximation (Eckart–Young) and image compression

    Mathematics. The truncation A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^{\top} is a best rank-k approximation of A in both the operator norm (error \sigma_{k+1}) and the Frobenius norm (error (\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2)^{1/2}). The theorem can be stated here and proved in Week 9 once norms are available; the operator-norm case has a short proof using a dimension count (\ker B for rank-k B meets \mathrm{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_{k+1}) nontrivially).

    Python. Load a grayscale image as a matrix, plot the singular values on a log scale, and display reconstructions for k = 5, 20, 50 alongside the storage ratio k(m+n+1)/(mn). A suitable image is scipy.datasets.ascent() (requires the pooch package and a one-time download) or any photograph read with matplotlib.pyplot.imread. Including a fixed image file in notes/Week7-8/images/ avoids a network dependency at render time.

    SVD 3. Principal component analysis

    Mathematics. For a centered N\times k data matrix X, the right singular vectors are the eigenvectors of the sample covariance X^{\top}X/(N-1), ordered by variance \sigma_i^2/(N-1). Projecting onto \mathrm{span}(v_1,\ldots,v_r) gives the r-dimensional subspace minimizing total squared orthogonal distance to the data. This is a useful contrast with W05.1 least squares, which minimizes vertical distances only; for k=2, the first principal direction is the total-least-squares line, and the two fitted lines differ visibly.

    Python. A scatter plot of correlated two-dimensional Gaussian data showing the OLS line and the first principal axis. For a higher-dimensional example, project a standard data set (e.g. the iris data, available through sklearn.datasets or as a small CSV) onto its first two principal components.

    SVD 4. The pseudoinverse and rank-deficient least squares

    Mathematics. W05.1 assumes X has full column rank so that X^{\top}X is invertible. When it does not, the solution set of the normal equations is an affine subspace, and M = X^{+}Y with X^{+} = V\Sigma^{+}U^{\top} is its unique element of minimum norm, the one lying in (\ker X)^{\perp}. The projection onto the column space is XX^{+} = U_rU_r^{\top} in all cases. The SVD also provides orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces (\mathrm{image}\,A, \ker A^{\top}, \mathrm{image}\,A^{\top}, \ker A), which connects to the kernel–range material of W01.3 and the adjoint orthogonality relations of W05.2.

    Python. A regression with a duplicated (or exactly collinear) predictor column; compare np.linalg.pinv with np.linalg.lstsq, and show that adding a vector from \ker X to the solution leaves the residual unchanged while increasing the norm. Plotting singular values also illustrates numerical rank when the collinearity is only approximate.

    SVD 5. Orthogonal Procrustes and point-cloud alignment

    Mathematics. Given 3\times N matrices A and B of corresponding points (centered), the orthogonal R minimizing \|RA - B\|_F is R = UV^{\top}, where BA^{\top} = U\Sigma V^{\top}. Equivalently R is the orthogonal factor in the polar decomposition of BA^{\top}, so this example fits in the same lecture as the polar decomposition. Requiring R \in SO(3) gives instead R = U\,\mathrm{diag}(1,1,d)\,V^{\top} with d = \det(UV^{\top}) (the Kabsch algorithm). The proof reduces to maximizing \mathrm{tr}(R^{\top}BA^{\top}), which uses the trace from W02.3.

    Python. Generate a random point cloud, apply a random rotation and small noise, recover the rotation, and display both clouds before and after alignment with a 3D scatter plot.

    SVD 6 (optional). Latent semantic analysis

    Mathematics. The term–document matrix of a small corpus is approximated by its rank-2 or rank-3 truncation; documents and terms are then compared by cosine similarity in the reduced space, and documents sharing no words can become similar. This illustrates Eckart–Young on non-image data.

    Python. A hand-built corpus of 8–10 short sentences on two topics, with a scatter plot of documents in the first two singular coordinates. The example is effective but requires more setup than the others.

    Polar decomposition

    Throughout, A = WP with W orthogonal (unitary over \mathbb{C}) and P = (A^{\top}A)^{1/2} positive. From the SVD A = U\Sigma V^{\top} one has W = UV^{\top} and P = V\Sigma V^{\top}. The square root P is the one defined by the spectral theorem and positivity in W05.2.

    Polar 1. The analogy with z = e^{i\theta}r

    Mathematics. For 1\times 1 complex matrices the polar decomposition is the polar form of a complex number, and in general unitary operators play the role of the unit circle and positive operators the role of the nonnegative reals. This analogy gives several short results. |\det A| = \det P. W and P commute if and only if A is normal, which corresponds to the fact that complex multiplication is commutative. For invertible A the factors are unique. Over \R, \det W = \operatorname{sign}\det A.

    Python. Not needed. This works best as a remark or a set of exercises.

    Polar 2. Nearest orthogonal matrix and re-orthonormalization

    Mathematics. For invertible A, the orthogonal factor W is the orthogonal matrix closest to A in the Frobenius norm. This is the case B = I of the Procrustes problem (SVD 5). It is also closest in the operator norm, since \|A - W\| = \max_i|\sigma_i - 1|. A practical setting is a rotation matrix that has been updated by many floating-point multiplications, or by numerical integration of \dot R = \Omega R (connecting to the matrix exponential in W04.3). Such a matrix drifts away from O(3), and replacing it with its polar factor is the standard correction. Gram–Schmidt (the Q of QR) also restores orthogonality, but the result depends on the order of the columns and is not the closest orthogonal matrix.

    Python. Multiply many slightly perturbed rotations, then plot \|R^{\top}R - I\| against the number of steps. Correct the result with the polar factor and with the Q from QR, and compare \|R - R_{\text{corrected}}\|_F for the two methods.

    Polar 3. Symmetric (Löwdin) orthogonalization

    Mathematics. Let X be a matrix whose columns x_1,\ldots,x_k are a linearly independent basis. Then X(X^{\top}X)^{-1/2} is the orthogonal factor of X. Its columns form the orthonormal set minimizing \sum_i\|x_i - u_i\|^2, and unlike Gram–Schmidt the construction treats all the vectors symmetrically. It is used in quantum chemistry to orthonormalize atomic orbitals, and it contrasts naturally with the Gram–Schmidt construction of W05.1. In the inner product notation of W05.1, X^{\top}X is the Gram matrix [J] restricted to the span of the columns.

    Python. Take three nearly orthogonal vectors in \R^3. Orthonormalize them by Gram–Schmidt in two different orders and by the Löwdin formula, then report \sum\|x_i - u_i\|^2 for each result.

    Polar 4. Continuum mechanics: rotation and stretch

    Mathematics. In elasticity the local deformation of a body is described by its deformation gradient F, and the polar decomposition F = RU splits it into a rotation R and a symmetric positive “stretch tensor” U. Measures of strain such as U - I and \tfrac12(F^{\top}F - I) depend only on U, so they are unchanged by rigid rotations. The eigenvectors of U are the principal stretch directions, and its eigenvalues are the principal stretches, which are the singular values of F.

    Python. Draw a square grid, its image under U (pure stretch along orthogonal axes, with the eigenvectors drawn), and its image under F = RU. A shear matrix such as \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} is a good example, because the decomposition shows that a shear is a stretch followed by a rotation.

    Polar 5. Interpolating transformations

    Mathematics. To interpolate between I and an invertible A, the straight-line path (1-t)I + tA can pass through singular or strongly flattened matrices when A contains a rotation. For example, with A a rotation by \pi the path passes through 0. Writing A = WP, one can interpolate the rotation through the exponential, W(t) = \exp(t\log W) for W \in SO(n), and the positive part through P(t) = (1-t)I + tP or \exp(t\log P). The resulting path stays invertible and moves without distortion. This method is used in computer animation (Shoemake and Duff, “Matrix animation and polar decomposition”, 1992), and it uses W04.3 (exponential) together with W05.2 (logarithm of a positive operator via the spectral theorem).

    Python. Animate, or show as a row of frames, the image of the unit square under the linear path and under the polar path, with A a rotation by 150^{\circ} composed with a stretch. Here scipy.linalg.polar, expm and logm provide everything required.

    Polar 6. Newton iteration for the orthogonal factor

    Mathematics. For invertible A, the iteration X_0 = A, X_{k+1} = \tfrac12\left(X_k + X_k^{-\top}\right) converges quadratically to W. In terms of the SVD, each singular value evolves as the scalar Newton iteration \sigma \mapsto \tfrac12(\sigma + \sigma^{-1}) for \sqrt{1}, while U and V stay fixed. This shows how the SVD reduces a matrix iteration to a scalar one.

    Python. Tabulate \|X_k^{\top}X_k - I\| for k = 0,\ldots,8 and compare the limit with scipy.linalg.polar.

    Polar 7. Topology of GL_n

    Mathematics. The map (W,P)\mapsto WP is a homeomorphism from O(n)\times\{\text{positive definite}\} to GL_n(\R). The positive definite matrices form a convex set, so GL_n(\R) deformation retracts onto O(n) via WP \mapsto W P^{1-t} (or W((1-t)P + tI)). It follows that GL_n(\R) has exactly two path components, distinguished by the sign of the determinant. Because U(n) is connected, GL_n(\C) is connected. These facts are standard at the graduate level, and the proof uses only the existence and uniqueness of the decomposition.

    Python. Not needed.

Matrix Decompositions

We will discuss some consequences of our discussion of unitary matrices, orthogonal matrices, and the spectral theorem that occur in applications.

The QR decomposition

Let X be an n\times k matrix with real or complex entries. The QR decomposition says that (assuming the columns of X are linearly independent) we can find an n\times n orthogonal (or unitary) matrix Q and an n\times k upper triangular matrix R such that X = QR.

This is just a version of the Gram–Schmidt algorithm. Gram–Schmidt applied to the columns x_1,\ldots, x_k of X yields an orthonormal sequence of vectors u_1,\ldots, u_k where (setting U to the matrix with columns equal to the u_{i}): U = XS

where S is k\times k and upper triangular. (See the Gram–Schmidt process). Then X=US^{-1}. Extend U by adding orthonormal columns to make it an orthogonal n\times n matrix Q and add zero rows to S^{-1} so that it becomes an upper triangular n\times k matrix.

The Polar Decomposition

The polar decomposition is, in some sense, a generalization of the “polar form” of a complex number, where z\not=0 has a unique representation z=re^{i\theta}.

Recall that a self-adjoint linear operator L on an inner product space V is positive if \langle Lv,v\rangle\ge 0 for all v\in V.

Suppose that L is a positive self-adjoint linear operator on an n-dimensional (real or complex) inner product space V.

Under these hypotheses, L is unitarily (or orthogonally) diagonalizable with distinct nonnegative real eigenvalues \lambda_{i} and has a spectral resolution

L = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_{i}p_{i}

where p_{i} is the orthogonal projection onto the \lambda_{i}-eigenspace of L.

Definition: The square root B=\sqrt{L} of L is the operator B = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}p_{i}

(taking the nonnegative square roots).

Using the properties of the p_{i} one checks immediately that B^{2}=L. It is straightforward to see that B is positive, self-adjoint, and is the unique positive operator whose square is L.

Also, the square root B is a positive, self-adjoint operator in its own right.

Now suppose A is any linear operator A:V\to V. Then A^{*}A:V\to V is self-adjoint and positive since \langle A^{*}Av,v\rangle = \langle Av, Av\rangle = \|Av\|^2\ge 0.

Proposition: L is positive if and only if L=A^{*}A for some operator A.

For example, the matrix D=X^{\top}X that came up in the equations for least squares regression is positive.

Now suppose that L is any linear operator L:V\to V where V is a real or complex inner product space. We will establish the following proposition.

Proposition (The Polar Decomposition): Let L:V\to V be a linear operator on a real or complex inner product space. Then there exist a positive operator P and a unitary (or orthogonal) operator U such that L=UP. Here P is unique and, if L is invertible, U is unique.

Alternatively, there is a positive operator P' and a unitary (or orthogonal) operator U' such that L=P'U' where P' is unique and U' is unique if L is invertible.

To construct U and P from L, notice first that if P and U exist, then because L^{*}L=P^{*}U^{*}UP=P^2 we must have P=\sqrt{L^{*}L} by the uniqueness of positive square roots. From the equation L(v)=UP(v)

we see that if w\in\mathrm{image}(P), so w=Pv, then we can define U(w)=L(v).

This is well-defined. To see this, observe that \|P(v)\|^2=\langle P(v),P(v)\rangle = \langle P^2(v),v\rangle = \langle L^{*}L(v),v\rangle = \|L(v)\|^2.

Thus if w=Pv_1=Pv_2, then P(v_1-v_2)=0, so L(v_1-v_2)=0 by the norm identity we just proved.

Even more, if w=Pv then \|Uw\|^2=\|UPv\|^2=\|L(v)\|^2=\|P(v)\|^2=\|w\|^2 so U is an isometry on the image of P.

From the construction above, we see that U is an isometry from the image of P to the image of L. Therefore these two spaces have the same dimension. Choose an orthonormal basis for \mathrm{image}(P), say u_1,\ldots, u_h. The value of U is determined on these u_{i} and U(u_1),\ldots, U(u_{h}) is a basis for \mathrm{image}(L). Extend the basis \{u_{i}\} of \mathrm{image}(P) to an orthonormal basis of V by adding u_{h+1},\ldots, u_{n}. Then choose an orthonormal basis w_{h+1},\ldots, w_{n} for \mathrm{image}(L)^{\perp}. We can define U on all of V by the definition above on \mathrm{image}(P) and then U(u_{j})=w_{j} for j=h+1,\ldots, n. This gives us an isometry (equivalently a unitary/orthogonal operator) on all of V that satisfies L=UP.

In the special case where L is invertible, P must also be invertible and then U is determined so P and U are unique in this case.

Proposition: Over the complex numbers, a unitary operator U has the form \exp(iJ) where J is a self-adjoint operator.

Proof: U is unitarily diagonalizable and its eigenvalues are all complex numbers of absolute value 1, hence of the form \exp(i\theta) where \theta is real. So let J be the operator that acts by \theta on the eigenspace where U acts by \exp(i\theta). Then U=\exp(iJ) and since J is unitarily diagonalizable with real eigenvalues it is self-adjoint.

## Overview

Consequences of the spectral theorem and of our study of unitary and orthogonal matrices that are important in applications:

- the $LU$ decomposition (Gaussian elimination, for reference);
- the $QR$ decomposition, with applications to least squares and to computing eigenvalues;
- the polar decomposition $L=UP$.

# The LU Decomposition

## The LU Decomposition

**Proposition.** Let $A$ be an $n\times m$ matrix over any field. Then there exist

- an $n\times n$ permutation matrix $P$,
- an $n\times n$ lower triangular matrix $L$,
- an $n\times m$ upper triangular matrix $U$ ($u_{ij}=0$ when $i>j$),

such that
$$
PA=LU.
$$

This has nothing to do with the spectral theorem: it is a restatement of Gaussian elimination.

## Proof: Gaussian Elimination

Apply row operations to $A$ until it is upper triangular. Generically each diagonal entry in turn serves as a pivot, and each row operation is left multiplication by an elementary matrix $E_i$ that is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal:
$$
E_kE_{k-1}\cdots E_1A=U,\qquad L=(E_kE_{k-1}\cdots E_1)^{-1}.
$$

- In general, rows may need to be permuted to get a nonzero pivot; collect these into $P$ and replace $A$ by $PA$.
- If $A$ is singular (or not square), $U$ may have zeros on its diagonal.

# The QR Decomposition

## The QR Decomposition

Let $X$ be an $n\times k$ matrix with real or complex entries and linearly independent columns.

**Proposition.** There is an $n\times n$ orthogonal (or unitary) matrix $Q$ and an $n\times k$ upper triangular matrix $R$ such that
$$
X=QR.
$$

This is just a version of the Gram--Schmidt algorithm.

## QR from Gram--Schmidt

Gram--Schmidt applied to the columns $x_1,\ldots,x_k$ of $X$ gives orthonormal $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ with $u_j\in\operatorname{span}(x_1,\ldots,x_j)$. With $U=[u_1\ \cdots\ u_k]$,
$$
U=XS,\qquad S\ \text{$k\times k$ upper triangular},
$$
so $X=US^{-1}$.

- Extend $U$ by orthonormal columns to an orthogonal/unitary $n\times n$ matrix $Q$.
- Add $n-k$ zero rows to $S^{-1}$ to get an $n\times k$ upper triangular $R$.

Then $X=QR$.

## Application: Least Squares

Given an $N\times k$ data matrix $X$ (rows are samples, columns are features) and targets $Y\in\mathbb{R}^N$, we model
$$
y_i=\sum_{j=1}^k m_jx_{ij}+b+\epsilon_i.
$$

Let $M=(m_1,\ldots,m_k,b)^\top$ and let $X'$ be $X$ with a column of $1$'s appended. Then
$$
Y=X'M+\epsilon.
$$

The $M$ minimizing $S(M)=\|Y-X'M\|^2$ solves the **normal equations**
$$
(X')^\top Y=(X')^\top X'M.
$$

## Least Squares via QR

Use Gram--Schmidt to write $X'=UK$, where $U$ has orthonormal columns and $K$ is $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ upper triangular. Then
$$
(X')^\top X'=K^\top U^\top UK=K^\top K,
$$
since $U^\top U=I$.

If the columns of $X'$ are independent, $K$ is invertible, and the normal equations become
$$
U^\top Y=KM.
$$

Since $K$ is upper triangular, solve by **back substitution**.

# The QR Algorithm

## Motivation: Power Iteration

Let $A$ be a positive real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\cdots>\lambda_n$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $u_1,\ldots,u_n$.

Choose a random unit vector $v_0$ and iterate
$$
v_{k+1}=\frac{Av_k}{\|Av_k\|}.
$$

Then $v_k$ converges (up to sign) to $u_1$, the eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$.

## Why Power Iteration Works

Write $v_0=\sum_i a_iu_i$ with $a_1=\langle v_0,u_1\rangle\neq 0$. Then $v_k$ is the unit vector in the direction of
$$
\begin{aligned}
A^kv_0&=\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\lambda_i^ku_i\\
&=\lambda_1^k\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\left(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\right)^ku_i
=\lambda_1^k(a_1u_1+e_k),
\end{aligned}
$$
where $e_k\to 0$ at least as fast as $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^k$.

So $v_k$ aligns with $u_1$ as $k\to\infty$.

## The QR Algorithm

Same idea, but apply Gram--Schmidt to *all* columns of $A^k$, so each column converges to an eigenvector.

**Algorithm.** Let $A_0=A$ be $n\times n$ real symmetric. For $k\ge 0$:

1. Compute the $QR$ decomposition $A_k=Q_kR_k$.
2. Set $A_{k+1}=R_kQ_k$.

Since $A_{k+1}=Q_k^{-1}A_kQ_k$, all the $A_k$ are orthogonally similar to $A$ and have the same eigenvalues.

## Accumulated Factors

Put
$$
\overline{Q}_k=Q_0Q_1\cdots Q_{k-1},\qquad
\overline{R}_k=R_{k-1}R_{k-2}\cdots R_0.
$$
Then
$$
A_k=\overline{Q}_k^\top A\,\overline{Q}_k
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
A^k=\overline{Q}_k\overline{R}_k.
$$

- $\overline{Q}_k$ is Gram--Schmidt applied to the columns of $A^k$.
- $A_k$ converges to a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues of $A$.
- $\overline{Q}_k$ converges to a matrix whose columns are eigenvectors of $A$.

## Example

$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}
2.1948 & 0.5638\\
0.5638 & 2.6052
\end{pmatrix}
$$

- The level curves $u^\top A_ku=1$, $u=(x,y)^\top$, are ellipses, each a rotation of the previous one.
- As $A_k$ becomes diagonal, the axes of the ellipse line up with the $x$- and $y$-axes.
- The off-diagonal entry of $A_k$ decays like $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^k$: its logarithm decreases linearly with slope $\log(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)$.

## Convergence

**Proposition.** Let $A$ be a positive definite real symmetric $n\times n$ matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1>\lambda_2>\cdots>\lambda_n$. Then $A_k\to\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)$, with rate $O(\rho^k)$, where
$$
\rho=\max_j\frac{\lambda_{j+1}}{\lambda_j}.
$$

We sketch why, assuming (generically) that $V^{-1}$ below has an $LU$ decomposition with no permutation.

## Sketch of Proof (1)

Write $A=V\Lambda V^\top=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ with $V$ orthogonal, and $V^{-1}=LU$. Then
$$
A^k=V\Lambda^kLU=V\left(\Lambda^kL\Lambda^{-k}\right)\Lambda^kU.
$$

The $(i,j)$ entry of $\Lambda^kL\Lambda^{-k}$ is $\ell_{ij}(\lambda_i/\lambda_j)^k$. For $i>j$ this ratio is $<1$, so
$$
\Lambda^kL\Lambda^{-k}=I+E_k,\qquad E_k=O(\rho^k).
$$

## Sketch of Proof (2)

Take the $QR$ decomposition $I+E_k=\tilde{Q}_k\tilde{R}_k$. Since Gram--Schmidt is continuous and $I=I\cdot I$,
$$
\tilde{Q}_k\to I,\qquad \tilde{R}_k\to I.
$$

Then
$$
A^k=\underbrace{V\tilde{Q}_k}_{\text{orthogonal}}\ \underbrace{\tilde{R}_k\Lambda^kU}_{\text{upper triangular}}
$$
is a $QR$ decomposition of $A^k$.

## Sketch of Proof (3)

The $QR$ decomposition is unique up to the signs of the columns, so
$$
\overline{Q}_k=V\tilde{Q}_kD_k,\qquad D_k=\operatorname{diag}(\pm1).
$$
Therefore
$$
\begin{aligned}
A_k=\overline{Q}_k^\top A\,\overline{Q}_k
&=D_k\tilde{Q}_k^\top(V^\top AV)\tilde{Q}_kD_k\\
&=D_k\tilde{Q}_k^\top\Lambda\tilde{Q}_kD_k.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $\tilde{Q}_k\to I$ and $D_k\Lambda D_k=\Lambda$, we get $A_k\to\Lambda$. $\square$

# The Polar Decomposition

## Polar Form

For a complex number $z\neq 0$,
$$
z=re^{i\theta},\qquad r>0,
$$
with $\theta$ determined up to a multiple of $2\pi$.

The polar decomposition generalizes this to linear operators:
$$
L=UP,\qquad P\ \text{positive},\quad U\ \text{unitary}.
$$

## Square Roots of Positive Operators

Recall that a self-adjoint $L$ is **positive** if $\langle Lv,v\rangle\ge 0$ for all $v$.

Such an $L$ is unitarily (orthogonally) diagonalizable with nonnegative eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k$ and spectral resolution
$$
L=\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_ip_i,
$$
where $p_i$ is the orthogonal projection onto the $\lambda_i$-eigenspace.

**Definition.** The square root of $L$ is
$$
\sqrt{L}=\sum_{i=1}^k\sqrt{\lambda_i}\,p_i\qquad(\sqrt{\lambda_i}\ge 0).
$$

Then $(\sqrt{L})^2=L$, and $\sqrt{L}$ is the unique positive operator whose square is $L$.

## Positive Operators are $A^*A$

For any linear $A:V\to V$, $A^*A$ is self-adjoint and positive:
$$
\langle A^*Av,v\rangle=\langle Av,Av\rangle=\|Av\|^2\ge 0.
$$
Conversely, if $L$ is positive and $A=\sqrt{L}$, then $L=A^*A$.

**Proposition.** $L$ is positive if and only if $L=A^*A$ for some operator $A$.

**Example.** The matrix $X^\top X$ from least squares regression is positive.

## The Polar Decomposition

**Proposition.** Let $L:V\to V$ be a linear operator on a finite-dimensional real or complex inner product space. Then there exist a positive $P$ and a unitary (orthogonal) $U$ with
$$
L=UP.
$$
$P$ is unique, and if $L$ is invertible, $U$ is unique.

Similarly $L=P'U'$ with $P'$ positive and $U'$ unitary (orthogonal); $P'$ is unique, and $U'$ is unique if $L$ is invertible.

## Constructing $P$

If $L=UP$, then
$$
L^*L=P^*U^*UP=P^2,
$$
so by uniqueness of positive square roots,
$$
P=\sqrt{L^*L}.
$$

## Constructing $U$ on $\image(P)$

If $w=Pv\in\image(P)$, define $U(w)=L(v)$.

**Well-defined:**
$$
\|Pv\|^2=\langle P^2v,v\rangle=\langle L^*Lv,v\rangle=\|Lv\|^2,
$$
so $Pv_1=Pv_2$ implies $L(v_1-v_2)=0$.

**Linear:** $U(aPv+Pv')=aLv+Lv'=aU(Pv)+U(Pv')$.

**Isometry:** $\|Uw\|=\|Lv\|=\|Pv\|=\|w\|$.

So $U$ is an isometry from $\image(P)$ onto $\image(L)$, and these have the same dimension $h$.

## Extending $U$ to $V$

- Choose an orthonormal basis $u_1,\ldots,u_h$ of $\image(P)$; then $U(u_1),\ldots,U(u_h)$ is an orthonormal basis of $\image(L)$.
- Extend to an orthonormal basis $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ of $V$.
- Choose an orthonormal basis $w_{h+1},\ldots,w_n$ of $\image(L)^\perp$.
- Set $U(u_j)=w_j$ for $j>h$.

$U$ carries an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis, so it is unitary (orthogonal), and $L=UP$.

If $L$ is invertible, so is $P$, and $U=LP^{-1}$ is determined.

## Unitary Operators are Exponentials

**Proposition.** Over $\mathbb{C}$, every unitary $U$ has the form $U=\exp(iJ)$ with $J$ self-adjoint.

**Proof.** $U$ is unitarily diagonalizable with eigenvalues of absolute value $1$, i.e. of the form $e^{i\theta}$ with $\theta$ real. Let $J$ act by $\theta$ on the eigenspace where $U$ acts by $e^{i\theta}$. Then $U=\exp(iJ)$, and $J$ is unitarily diagonalizable with real eigenvalues, hence self-adjoint. $\square$

So $L=UP=e^{iJ}P$ is the operator analogue of $z=e^{i\theta}r$.

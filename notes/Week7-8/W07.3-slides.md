## Overview

- The singular value decomposition (SVD): a version of the spectral theorem for non-square matrices.
- Low rank approximation, with an application to image compression.
- Norms on vector spaces; all norms on $\R^n$ or $\C^n$ are equivalent.
- The operator norm on matrices.

# The Singular Value Decomposition

## Setup

Let $X$ be a real $n\times m$ matrix and let $D=X^{\top}X$.

- $D$ is a symmetric, positive semidefinite $m\times m$ matrix.
- By the spectral theorem there is an orthogonal $P$ with
$$
P^{\top}DP=\Lambda,\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad DP=P\Lambda,
$$
so the columns of $P$ are orthonormal eigenvectors for $D$.

Set $A=XP$. Then
$$
A^{\top}A=P^{\top}X^{\top}XP=\Lambda,
$$
so the columns of $A$ are orthogonal, but not necessarily orthonormal.

Geometrically, row $i$ of $A$ gives the coordinates of row $i$ of $X$ in the orthonormal basis given by the columns of $P$.

## Normalizing the Columns

**Full rank case.** If $D$ is positive definite (all $\lambda_i>0$), which happens exactly when $X$ has rank $m\le n$, set
$$
A_1=A\Lambda^{-1/2}.
$$
Then $A_1^{\top}A_1=\Lambda^{-1/2}\Lambda\Lambda^{-1/2}=I$, so $A_1$ has orthonormal columns.

**General case.** Order the eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_m$, with $\lambda_{r+1}=\cdots=\lambda_m=0$, so $X$ has rank $r$.

- Columns $r+1,\ldots,m$ of $A$ are zero; drop them to get $A_r$.
- Shrink $\Lambda$ to the $r\times r$ diagonal matrix $\Lambda_r$ of nonzero eigenvalues.
- $A_1=A_r\Lambda_r^{-1/2}$ is $n\times r$ with orthonormal columns.

## Building $U$ and $\Sigma$

- Complete the columns of $A_1$ to an orthogonal $n\times n$ matrix $U$ by adding orthonormal columns $u_{r+1},\ldots,u_n$.
- Let $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ and let $\Sigma$ be the $n\times m$ "diagonal" matrix of these *singular values*.

In $U\Sigma$:

- the first $r$ columns are $A_1\Lambda_r^{1/2}$, which are the first $r$ columns of $A=XP$;
- the remaining columns are zero, as are the remaining columns of $XP$.

## The SVD

**Theorem.** Therefore $XP=U\Sigma$, or
$$
X=U\Sigma V^{\top}
$$
where $U$ ($n\times n$) and $V=P$ ($m\times m$) are orthogonal and $\Sigma$ is $n\times m$ "diagonal."

The singular values (the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$) are uniquely determined.

**Reduced SVD.** Keep the first $r$ columns of $U$ ($U_r$, $n\times r$), the $r\times r$ block $\Sigma_r$, and the first $r$ columns of $V$ ($V_r$, $m\times r$):
$$
X=U_r\Sigma_rV_r^{\top}.
$$

# Low Rank Approximation

## A Sum of Rank One Maps

If $X$ is the matrix of $L:\R^m\to\R^n$, the reduced SVD says
$$
X=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top},
$$
where $u_i$, $v_i$ are the columns of $U$, $V$.

- $u_iv_i^{\top}$ is an $(n\times 1)(1\times m)$ product, an $n\times m$ matrix.
- It has rank one: its range is spanned by $u_i$.

## Truncating the Sum

$$
X_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i u_i v_i^{\top}
$$

- If $\sigma_i>0$ for $i\le k$, then $X_k$ has rank $k$.
- We will show $X_k$ is the *best* approximation to $X$ among matrices of rank at most $k$.
- There are several meanings of "best"; the most useful in applications: $X_k$ minimizes the squared error
$$
E(B,X)=\sum_{i,j}(x_{ij}-b_{ij})^2
$$
over all matrices $B=(b_{ij})$ of rank at most $k$.

## Application: Image Compression

- A gray-scale image is an array of integers, one per pixel ($0$ for black, $255$ for white).
- A color image is three such arrays (red, green, blue).

Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ gray-scale image.

- As $k$ increases, $A_k$ gets close to $A$, so it represents "nearly" the same image.
- Storing $A$ takes $n^2$ numbers.
- Storing $A_k$ takes $k$ columns of $U$, $k$ columns of $V$, and $k$ singular values:
$$
2kn+k=k(2n+1)\ \text{numbers}.
$$

# Norms

## Norms on Vector Spaces

Let $V$ be a vector space over $F=\R$ or $\C$. A **norm** on $V$ is a function $\|\cdot\|:V\to\R$ such that:

- $\|v\|\ge 0$, with equality if and only if $v=0$;
- $\|v+w\|\le\|v\|+\|w\|$;
- $\|cv\|=|c|\|v\|$ for $c\in F$.

Dropping "$\|v\|=0$ only if $v=0$" gives a **seminorm**.

A norm makes $V$ a metric space with $d(v,w)=\|v-w\|$.

## Two Key Examples

**The sup norm** relative to a basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$:
$$
\supnorm{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ie_i}=\max\{|a_1|,\ldots,|a_n|\}.
$$

**The $\ell^2$ (Euclidean) norm** from an inner product:
$$
\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}.
$$
For the usual dot product, this gives Euclidean distance.

## The Triangle Inequality for $\ell^2$

Cauchy--Schwarz: $|\langle v,w\rangle|\le\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle\langle w,w\rangle}$. So
$$\begin{aligned}
\|v+w\|^2&=\langle v,v\rangle+2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle v,w\rangle+\langle w,w\rangle\\
&\le\|v\|^2+2|\langle v,w\rangle|+\|w\|^2\\
&\le\|v\|^2+2\|v\|\|w\|+\|w\|^2=(\|v\|+\|w\|)^2.
\end{aligned}$$
Take square roots.

## Equivalent Norms

**Definition.** Norms $\|\cdot\|_1$ and $\|\cdot\|_2$ on $V$ are **equivalent** if there are constants $A,B>0$ with
$$
A\|v\|_1\le\|v\|_2\le B\|v\|_1\quad\text{for all }v\in V.
$$

**Proposition.** All norms on $V=\R^n$ or $\C^n$ are equivalent.

Strategy: show any norm $\rho$ is equivalent to the sup norm for the standard basis.

## The Upper Bound

Let $\rho$ be any norm and $v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ie_i$. Then
$$\begin{aligned}
\rho(v)&\le\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_i|\rho(e_i)\\
&\le\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho(e_i)\Bigr)\supnorm{v}.
\end{aligned}$$
So $\rho(v)\le B\supnorm{v}$ with $B=\sum_i\rho(e_i)$.

**Lemma.** $\rho$ is continuous for the metric given by $\supnorm{\cdot}$:
$$
|\rho(x)-\rho(y)|\le\rho(x-y)\le B\supnorm{x-y}.
$$

This much works for seminorms too. The next step needs $\rho(v)=0\Rightarrow v=0$.

## Compactness of the Sphere

**Lemma.** The unit sphere $S=\{v:\supnorm{v}=1\}$ is compact.

**Proof.**

- The closed ball $\{\supnorm{v}\le 1\}$ is a finite product of intervals $[-1,1]$ (or closed disks $|z|\le 1$), so it is compact.
- The open ball $\{\supnorm{v}<1\}$ is a finite product of open intervals (or open disks), so it is open.
- $S$ is the closed ball minus the open ball, so it is closed, hence compact. $\square$

## The Lower Bound

$\rho$ is continuous on the compact set $S$, so it attains a minimum $A$, and $A>0$ since $\rho$ is nonzero on $S$.

For $v\neq 0$:
$$
\rho\Bigl(\frac{v}{\supnorm{v}}\Bigr)\ge A
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\rho(v)\ge A\supnorm{v}.
$$

Altogether:
$$
A\supnorm{v}\le\rho(v)\le B\supnorm{v}.\qquad\square
$$

## Remarks and Consequences

- The key input is compactness of closed intervals in $\R$ or closed disks in $\C$ (Heine--Borel).
- Conrad, [Equivalence of Norms](http://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/gradnumthy/equivnorms.pdf): all norms on a finite dimensional vector space over a complete valued field are equivalent.

**Corollary.** For finite dimensional $V$, $W$ over $\R$ or $\C$:

- every linear map $V\to W$ is continuous in any norm topology;
- bounded, open, closed and compact sets are the same for every norm.

## Infinite Dimensions Are Different

On $C([0,1])$ consider
$$
\supnorm{f}=\sup_{0\le x\le 1}|f(x)|,\qquad
\|f\|_{L^1}=\int_0^1|f(x)|\,dx.
$$

A spike of height $a$ and area $1$ has $\supnorm{f}=a$ and $\|f\|_{L^1}=1$, so these norms are not equivalent.

# Matrix Norms

## Matrices as Vectors

$M_{n\times m}(F)\cong F^{nm}$, so matrices have a sup norm $\supnorm{\cdot}$ and an $\ell^2$ norm, and all norms on matrices are equivalent.

For $A\in M_n(F)$ let
$$
\rho_A(v)=\|Av\|\qquad(\ell^2\text{ norm on }F^n).
$$
$\rho_A$ is a seminorm:

- $\rho_A(v)\ge 0$;
- $\rho_A(v+w)=\|Av+Aw\|\le\rho_A(v)+\rho_A(w)$;
- $\rho_A(av)=|a|\rho_A(v)$.

So $\rho_A$ is continuous, and $\rho_A(v)\le C\|v\|$ for some $C\ge 0$.

## The Operator Norm

**Proposition.** There is a smallest $b\ge 0$ with $\rho_A(v)\le b\|v\|$ for all $v$.

**Proof.** $\rho_A$ is continuous on the compact sphere $\{\|v\|=1\}$, so it attains a maximum $b$. $\square$

**Definition.** The **operator norm** of $A$ is
$$
\|A\|=\max_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|.
$$

## Properties of the Operator Norm

**Proposition.** The operator norm is a norm on $M_n(F)$, and for $A,B\in M_n(F)$:

- $\|AB\|\le\|A\|\|B\|$;
- $\|A^{*}\|=\|A\|$;
- $\|A^{*}A\|=\|AA^{*}\|=\|A\|^2$;
- $|\langle Av,w\rangle|\le\|A\|\|v\|\|w\|$;
- $\supnorm{A}\le\|A\|\le n\sqrt{n}\,\supnorm{A}$.

Here $A^{*}=A^{\top}$ when $F=\R$.

So the operator norm gives $M_n(F)$ the usual topology of $F^{n^2}$, and $M_n(F)$ is complete for it.

(Conrad, [Computing the Norm of a Matrix](https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/matrixnorm.pdf), Theorem 3.4.)

## Proofs

**Products.** $\|ABv\|\le\|A\|\|Bv\|\le\|A\|\|B\|\|v\|$, and $\|AB\|$ is the smallest such constant.

**Inner products.** $|\langle Av,w\rangle|\le\|Av\|\|w\|\le\|A\|\|v\|\|w\|$ by Cauchy--Schwarz.

## Proof: the Adjoint

By Cauchy--Schwarz,
$$
\|Av\|^2=\langle Av,Av\rangle=\langle v,A^{*}Av\rangle\le\|v\|\|A^{*}Av\|,
$$
so
$$
\|A\|^2\le\|A^{*}A\|\le\|A^{*}\|\|A\|.
$$

- If $A\ne 0$, divide by $\|A\|$: $\|A\|\le\|A^{*}\|$.
- Apply this to $A^{*}$: $\|A^{*}\|\le\|A\|$. So they are equal.
- Then $\|A\|^2\le\|A^{*}A\|\le\|A\|^2$, so $\|A^{*}A\|=\|A\|^2$; similarly for $AA^{*}$. $\square$

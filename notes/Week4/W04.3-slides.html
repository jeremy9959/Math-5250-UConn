The Minimal Polynomial, Cyclic Subspaces, and Jordan Normal Form

Math 5250 — Week 4

Motivation

If f(x)=P_A(x) factors over an algebraically closed field as f(x)=\prod_i(x-\lambda_i)^{n_i} and all n_i=1, then A is diagonalizable.

If some n_i>1, the answer is open: sometimes A is diagonalizable, sometimes not.

To understand the general case, we need some theory of polynomials.

Polynomials over F[x]

F[x]: polynomials with coefficients in a field F (not assumed algebraically closed).

f(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n has degree \deg(f)=n if a_n\neq0, and is monic if a_n=1.

Proposition. (The Division Algorithm) Let f,g\in F[x]. There exist unique polynomials q(x),r(x), with 0\le\deg(r(x))<\deg(g(x)), such that f(x)=q(x)g(x)+r(x).

Greatest Common Divisors

Definition. u(x) is a divisor of f(x) if f(x)=u(x)v(x) for some v(x). h(x) is a common divisor of f(x) and g(x) if it divides both.

Proposition. (Euclid’s Algorithm) Given nonzero f(x),g(x)\in F[x], there is a unique nonzero monic polynomial d(x)=(f(x),g(x)) of minimal degree of the form a(x)f(x)+b(x)g(x). This is the greatest common divisor:

  1. d(x) is a common divisor of f(x) and g(x);
  2. every common divisor h(x) of f(x),g(x) divides d(x).

Roots of the GCD

Proposition. The roots of d(x)=(f(x),g(x)) are exactly the common roots of f(x) and g(x). If \lambda has multiplicity a in f and b in g, its multiplicity in d is the smaller of a and b.

Corollary. f(x) and g(x) have no common root in any algebraic closure of F if and only if there are polynomials a(x),b(x) with a(x)f(x)+b(x)g(x)=1.

Irreducible Polynomials

Definition. f(x) is irreducible if it cannot be factored as f(x)=g(x)h(x) with \deg(g),\deg(h)\ge1.

Proposition. If f(x) is irreducible and a(x)b(x) is a multiple of f(x), then either a(x) is a multiple of f(x) or b(x) is.

Unique Factorization

Proposition. A monic polynomial f(x) factors as f(x)=\prod_i p_i(x)^{n_i} where the p_i(x) are distinct monic irreducible polynomials, and the set of p_i and the n_i are unique up to order.

The Primary Decomposition

Let L:V\to V and factor the characteristic polynomial into distinct irreducibles: P_L(x)=p_1(x)^{n_1}\cdots p_k(x)^{n_k}.

Theorem. V decomposes as an internal direct sum V=V_1\oplus V_2\oplus\cdots\oplus V_k, where p_i(L)^{n_i}V_i=0 for each i.

The Minimal Polynomial

Suppose \dim V=n and L:V\to V is linear. Cayley–Hamilton gives P_L(L)V=0.

Definition. The minimal polynomial m_L(x) is the monic polynomial of smallest degree such that m_L(L)V=0.

Proposition. The minimal polynomial m_L(x) is a divisor of the characteristic polynomial P_L(x).

Cyclic Subspaces

Definition. Let \dim V=n. V is cyclic for L if there is a v\in V such that v,Lv,\ldots,L^{n-1}v span V. Such a v is called a generator for V.

If v generates V, there is a relation L^nv=-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_iL^iv, giving a polynomial q(x)=x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0 with q(L)V=0. This q(x) is the minimal polynomial of L acting on V.

The Companion Matrix

In the basis \{L^iv\}_{i=0}^{n-1}, the matrix of L is A=\begin{pmatrix} 0&0&0&\cdots&0&-a_0\\ 1&0&0&\cdots&0&-a_1\\ 0&1&0&\cdots&0&-a_2\\ 0&0&1&\cdots&0&-a_3\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\ 0&0&0&\cdots&1&-a_{n-1} \end{pmatrix}

Remark. A matrix of this shape for q(x) is called the companion matrix of q(x).

The Companion Matrix (cont.)

Proposition. The characteristic polynomial of the companion matrix A is q(x).

Corollary. If V is cyclic for L, then the minimal polynomial and characteristic polynomial of L coincide.

Decomposition into Cyclic Subspaces

Theorem. Let L:V\to V, where \dim V=n. Then there is a decomposition V=V_1\oplus V_2\oplus\cdots\oplus V_k into cyclic subspaces, where the minimal polynomial on V_i is a power p_i(x)^{k_i} of a monic irreducible polynomial over F. The polynomials p_i(x)^{k_i} are unique up to order.

Two Consequences

  • The minimal polynomial m_L(x) is the least common multiple of the p_i(x)^{k_i}.
  • The characteristic polynomial P_L(x) is the product of the p_i(x)^{k_i}.
  • Hence every irreducible factor of P_L(x) is a factor of m_L(x).

Proof: Setup

Start with the primary decomposition V=\bigoplus_i V_i, where p_i(L)^{n_i}V_i=0, the p_i(x) are distinct irreducibles, and P_L(x)=\prod_i p_i(x)^{n_i}.

It suffices to show: if p(x) is irreducible of degree d and p(L)^nV=0, then V splits as a direct sum of cyclic subspaces with minimal polynomials p(x)^{k_i}, \sum k_i=n.

Proof: Choosing a Generator

Choose v_1\in V so that p(L)^{k_1}v_1=0, p(L)^{k_1-1}v_1\neq0, with k_1 maximal among all such choices. Then p(L)^{k_1}V=0.

v_1 generates a cyclic subspace with basis v_1,Lv_1,\ldots,L^{k_1d-1}v_1.

These are independent: a dependence relation would give h(L)v_1=0 for some h(x) of degree <dk_1. The gcd s(x) of h(x) and p(x)^{k_1} also satisfies s(L)v_1=0; since p(x) is irreducible, s(x)=p(x)^k with k<k_1, contradicting the maximality of k_1.

Proof: Constructing the Complement

Let V_1 be generated by v_1. Goal: find an L-invariant W with V=V_1\oplus W; since W has smaller dimension and satisfies the same hypotheses, induction on \dim V then finishes the decomposition.

Construct W inductively: start with W_0=(0), and build L-invariant subspaces W_j with V_1\cap W_j=0, so that for j large enough, V_j:=V_1\oplus W_j=V.

Proof: Extending W_j

Suppose W_j is L-invariant with V_1\cap W_j=0. Choose w\in V, w\notin V_j.

Since p(L)^{k_1}w=0\in V_j, and by Euclid’s algorithm, the set of k(x) with k(L)w\in V_j is exactly the multiples of some p(x)^f, with 1\le f\le k_1.

Write p(L)^fw=c(L)v_1+w_j,\qquad w_j\in W_j.

Proof: Extending W_j (cont.)

Applying p(L)^{k_1-f} to both sides gives 0=p(L)^{k_1-f}c(L)v_1+p(L)^{k_1-f}w_j. Since V_1\cap W_j=0, this forces p(L)^{k_1-f}c(L)v_1=0, so c(x) is divisible by p(x)^f: write c(x)=\delta(x)p(x)^f.

Set w'=w-\delta(L)v_1, let W' be the cyclic subspace generated by w', and set W_{j+1}=W_j+W'.

Proof: V_1\cap W_{j+1}=0

Suppose x=a(L)v_1=w_j+b(L)w'\in V_1\cap W_{j+1}. Since b(L)w=a(L)v_1-w_j+b(L)\delta(L)v_1\in V_j, we must have b(x)=p(x)^f\beta(x) for some \beta(x). Then b(L)w-b(L)\delta(L)v_1=\beta(L)\big(p(L)^fw-c(L)v_1\big)=\beta(L)w_j, so x=w_j+\beta(L)w_j\in V_1\cap W_j=0. Hence V_1\cap W_{j+1}=0.

Proof: Conclusion

Repeating this extension a finite number of times (since \dim V<\infty) produces an L-invariant W with V=V_1\oplus W.

Splitting off a cyclic subspace of W and continuing inductively completes the existence proof. (Uniqueness is omitted.)

The Jordan Normal Form

Suppose P_L(x)=\prod_i(x-\lambda_i)^{n_i} factors into linear factors — always true if F is algebraically closed, but not required.

The cyclic decomposition writes V=\bigoplus_i V_i, each V_i cyclic with minimal polynomial (x-\lambda_i)^{k_i}.

A Basis for a Cyclic Block

Let v_0 generate V_i. The vectors v_j=(L-\lambda_i)^jv_0,\qquad j=0,\ldots,k_i-1 form a basis for V_i, and Lv_j=v_{j+1}+\lambda_iv_j\ (j=0,\ldots,k_i-2),\qquad Lv_{k_i-1}=\lambda_iv_{k_i-1}.

In this basis, L|_{V_i} has matrix J_{k_i}(\lambda_i)=\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_i&0&0&\cdots&0&0\\ 1&\lambda_i&0&\cdots&0&0\\ 0&1&\lambda_i&\cdots&0&0\\ 0&0&1&\cdots&0&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\ 0&0&0&\cdots&1&\lambda_i \end{pmatrix}

Jordan Blocks

Definition. The k\times k matrix with \lambda on the diagonal, 1 on the subdiagonal, and 0 elsewhere is the Jordan block J_k(\lambda).

Theorem. (Jordan Normal Form) If the characteristic polynomial of L factors into linear factors, then there is a basis of V in which the matrix of L is block diagonal, with blocks J_{k_i}(\lambda_i), where (x-\lambda_i)^{k_i} are the minimal polynomials of the cyclic subspaces V_i.

Consequences of Jordan Form

Every matrix A whose characteristic polynomial factors into linear factors is a sum A=D+N where D is diagonal (eigenvalues repeated by multiplicity) and N is nilpotent (N^k=0 for some k).

In a single Jordan block of size k: \lambda I+N with N^k=0 but N^{k-1}\neq0.

Each Jordan block has a one-dimensional space of eigenvectors, so the number of independent eigenvectors equals the number of Jordan blocks.

## Polynomials over a Field

- Over an algebraically closed field, $f(x)=\prod_i (x-\lambda_i)^{n_i}$, where the $\lambda_i$ are the roots and $n_i$ is the multiplicity of $\lambda_i$.
- If $f(x)=P_A(x)$ and all $n_i=1$, then $A$ is diagonalizable.
- $F[x]$: polynomials with coefficients in $F$ (not necessarily algebraically closed). $f(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n$ has degree $n$ if $a_n\ne 0$, and is **monic** if $a_n=1$.

## The Division Algorithm

**Proposition (Division Algorithm).** For $f,g\in F[x]$, there exist unique polynomials $q(x),r(x)$, with $0\le \deg(r(x))<\deg(g(x))$, such that
$$
f(x) = q(x)g(x)+r(x).
$$

**Definition.** $u(x)$ is a **divisor** of $f(x)$ if $f(x)=u(x)v(x)$ for some $v(x)$. $h(x)$ is a **common divisor** of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ if it divides both.

## Euclid's Algorithm

**Proposition (Euclid's Algorithm).** Given nonzero $f(x),g(x)\in F[x]$, there is a unique nonzero monic polynomial $d(x)=(f(x),g(x))$ of minimal degree of the form $a(x)f(x)+b(x)g(x)$. This **greatest common divisor** satisfies:

1. $d(x)$ is a common divisor of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$;
2. if $h(x)$ is a common divisor of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$, then $h(x)$ divides $d(x)$.

## Roots of the GCD

**Proposition.** The roots of $d(x)=(f(x),g(x))$ are exactly the common roots of $f(x)$ and $g(x)$. If $\lambda$ is a common root of multiplicity $a$ in $f$ and $b$ in $g$, its multiplicity in $d(x)$ is $\min(a,b)$.

**Corollary.** $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ have no common root in an algebraic closure of $F$ if and only if $a(x)f(x)+b(x)g(x)=1$ for some polynomials $a(x),b(x)$.

## Irreducible Polynomials

**Definition.** $f(x)$ is **irreducible** if it cannot be factored as $f(x)=g(x)h(x)$ with $\deg(g),\deg(h)\ge 1$.

**Proposition.** If $f(x)$ is irreducible and $a(x)b(x)$ is a multiple of $f(x)$, then $a(x)$ is a multiple of $f(x)$ or $b(x)$ is.

## Unique Factorization

**Proposition.** A monic polynomial $f(x)$ factors as
$$
f(x) = \prod_{i} p_{i}(x)^{n_{i}}
$$
where the $p_i(x)$ are distinct monic irreducible polynomials, and the set of $p_i$ and the $n_i$ are unique up to order.

## Decomposition

Let $L:V\to V$ be linear and factor $P_L(x)$ into powers of distinct irreducibles:
$$
P_{L}(x) = p_{1}(x)^{n_{1}}\cdots p_{k}(x)^{n_{k}}.
$$

**Proposition.** $V$ decomposes as
$$
V = V_1 \oplus V_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_k
$$
where $p_{i}(L)^{n_{i}}V_i=0$ for each $i$.

## The Minimal Polynomial

Let $V$ be finite dimensional and $L:V\to V$ linear. By Cayley-Hamilton, $P_L(L)V=0$.

**Definition.** The **minimal polynomial** $m_L(x)$ is the monic polynomial of smallest degree such that $m_L(L)V=0$.

**Proposition.** $m_L(x)$ is a divisor of the characteristic polynomial $P_L(x)$.

## Cyclic Subspaces

**Definition.** A finite dimensional vector space $V$ of dimension $n$ is a **cyclic space** for $L:V\to V$ if there is $v\in V$ such that $L^{i}(v)$, $i=0,\ldots,n-1$, span $V$. Such a $v$ is a **generator** for $V$.

If $v$ is a generator, there is a relation $L^nv=-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_iL^iv$, giving a polynomial
$$
q(x) = x^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}
$$
with $q(L)V=0$; $q(x)$ is the minimal polynomial of $L$ on $V$.

## The Companion Matrix

In the basis $\{L^iv\}_{i=0}^{n-1}$, the matrix of $L$ is
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_{0} \\
1 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_{1} \\
0 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & -a_{2} \\
0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & -a_{3} \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & -a_{n-1}
\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Remark.** A matrix of this shape for $q(x)$ is called the **companion matrix** of $q(x)$.

**Proposition.** The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is $q(x)$.

**Corollary.** If $V$ is cyclic for $L$, then the minimal and characteristic polynomials of $L$ coincide.

## Decomposition into Cyclic Subspaces

**Theorem.** Let $L:V\to V$ be linear, where $V$ has dimension $n$ over $F$. Then there is a decomposition
$$
V = V_{1}\oplus V_{2}\oplus \cdots \oplus V_{k}
$$
into cyclic subspaces, where the minimal polynomial of $V_i$ is a power $p_i(x)^{k_i}$ of a monic irreducible polynomial over $F$. The $p_i(x)^{k_i}$ are unique up to order.

## Consequences of the Decomposition

- The minimal polynomial $m_L(x)$ of $V$ is the least common multiple of the $p_i(x)^{k_i}$.
- The characteristic polynomial of $V$ is the product of the characteristic polynomials of the $V_i$, i.e. the product of the $p_i(x)^{k_i}$.
- Every irreducible factor of $P_L(x)$ is a factor of the minimal polynomial of $L$.

## The Jordan Normal Form

Suppose $P_L(x)=\prod_i(x-\lambda_i)^{n_i}$ (always true if $F$ is algebraically closed, but not required). The cyclic decomposition writes $V$ as a sum of cyclic subspaces $V_i$ with minimal polynomial $(x-\lambda_i)^{k_i}$.

**Definition.** The $k\times k$ matrix with $\lambda$ on the diagonal, $1$ on the subdiagonal, and $0$ elsewhere is the **Jordan block** $J_k(\lambda)$:
$$
J_{k}(\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}
\lambda & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
1 & \lambda & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & \lambda & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1 & \lambda
\end{pmatrix}
$$

## The Jordan Normal Form (cont.)

**Theorem (Jordan Normal Form).** If the characteristic polynomial of $L$ factors into linear factors, there is a basis of $V$ in which the matrix of $L$ is block diagonal, with blocks $J_{k_i}(\lambda_i)$, where the $(x-\lambda_i)^{k_i}$ are the minimal polynomials of the cyclic subspaces $V_i$.

**Remark.** Equivalently, $A=D+N$ where $D$ is diagonal with the eigenvalues of $A$ (repeated by multiplicity) and $N$ is nilpotent ($N^k=0$ for some $k$). In a single Jordan block of dimension $k$, $A=\lambda I+N$ with $N^k=0$ but $N^{k-1}\ne 0$.

**Remark.** Each Jordan block has a one-dimensional space of eigenvectors, so the number of independent eigenvectors equals the number of Jordan blocks.

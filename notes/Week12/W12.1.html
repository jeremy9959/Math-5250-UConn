Bilinear Forms and Matrix Representations”

In this section we discuss bilinear forms over \mathbf{R} and \mathbf{C}. In some ways a bilinear form is a generalization of the notion of inner product, where we relax some of the conditions (such as symmetry or positive definiteness). For a thorough discussion of this topic, see Keith Conrad’s article Bilinear Forms.

In particular, we will consider only the case of vector spaces and bilinear forms over \mathbf{R} or \mathbf{C}. There are many interesting aspects over the theory over fields like \Q or finite fields; see Conrad’s notes for a discussion of this more general situation.

The definition doesn’t depend on the field F, however.

Definition: Let F be a field and V a vector space over F. A bilinear form on V is a function B:V\times V\to F that is linear in each variable when the other is fixed. In other words:

\begin{aligned} B(au+v,w)&=aB(u,w)+B(v,w)\\ B(u,av+w)&=aB(u,v)+B(u,w)\\ \end{aligned}

If B(v,w)=B(w,v) for all v,w\in V, we call B symmetric. If B(v,w)=-B(w,v) for all v,w\in V we call B antisymmetric or skew-symmetric.. If B(v,v)=0 for all v we call B alternating.

An inner product over a real vector space is a symmetric bilinear form. This isn’t true over a complex vector space, because we required such inner products to be hermitian..

Other examples:

  • If A\in M_{n}(F), then B(v,w)=v^{\top}Aw is a bilinear form.
  • If (u,v) and (a,b) are in F^{2}, then ub-va is a skew-symmetric bilinear form on $F^{2}.

Lemma: Over \mathbf{R} or \mathbf{C} (or indeed any field of characteristic different from 2), alternative forms are the same thing as skew-symmetric forms.

Proof: If B is alternating, then B(x+y,x+y)=0=B(x,y)+B(y,x) so B is skew-symmetric. If B is skew-symmetric then B(x,x)=-B(x,x) so 2B(x,x)=0, so B is alternating assuming F does not have characteristic two.

From now on, every vector space is over \mathbf{R} or \mathbf{C}.

Proposition: Every bilinear form B on V is expressible uniquely as a sum B(u,v)=B_1(u,v)+B_2(u,) for all u,v\in V where B_1 is symmetric and B_2 si skew-symmetric (alternating).

Proof: Let B_{1}(u,v) = \frac{B(u,v)+B(v,u)}{2} and B_{2}(u,v) = \frac{B(u,v)-B(v,u)}{2}.

Finally, we have the polarization identity: Suppose B is symmetric. Then B(x,y) = \frac{1}{2}(B(x+y,x+y)-B(x,x)-B(y,y))

So a symmetric bilinear form is determined by its values B(v,v) for all v\in V.

Orthogonality

We can generalize the notion of orthogonality from inner products to general bilinear forms by saying that w \perp v when B(w,v)=0 and, more generally, that given a subspace W\subset V, then v\perp W provided B(v,w)=0 for all w\in W.

In general, this relation need not be symmetric; for a general bilinear form, one can have B(v,w)=0 (so v\perp w) but B(w,v)\not=0 so w\not\perp v. For symmetric or alternating forms, however, the relation is symmetric since B(v,w)=\pm B(w,v) for all v,w\in V in these cases.

One can show (see Conrad, Proposition 1.17 (Bilinear Forms) that \perp is a symmetric relation if and only if B is either symmetric or alternating.

If B is symmetric or alternating, and W\subset V is a subspace, then just as we did earlier with inner products we define W^{\perp} = \{ v\in V: v \perp w\quad \forall w\in W\}

Since it is quite possible, depending on B, for some nonzero vectors v to be perpendicular to themselves, one can have W\cap W^{\perp}\not=0. For example if B is the alternating form B((x,y),(u,v))=xv-yu on F^{2}, and W is the span of (1,1), then W^{\perp}=W. In fact every v\in V is perpendicular to itself in this situation.

Notice that if W\subset V is a subspace, then the restriction of a bilinear form B on V is a bilinear form on W. Also, if V_1 and V_2 are vector spaces with bilinear forms B_1 and B_2, then one can construct a bilinear form B=B_1\oplus B_2 on V_1\oplus V_2 by setting B((x,y),(z,w))=B_1(x,z)+B_2(y,w).

In this construction V_1 and V_2 are orthogonal in the sum, and so this sum is called the orthogonal direct sum of B_1 and B_2. Also, the restriction of B to V_1 and V_2 recovers B_1 and B_2.

Bilinear forms and duality

Let V be a vector space over F with a bilinear form B. For fixed v\in V, the map f(w)=B(v,w) is a linear functional on V and hence an element of the dual space \Hom(V,F). This gives a linear map L_{B}: V\to \Hom(V,F).

Alternatively we can fix v\in V and define g_{v}(w)=B(w,v). This gives a map R_{B}:V\to \Hom(V,F).

Proposition: If V is finite dimensional, and we identify V with \Hom(V,\Hom(V,F)) then the two maps L_{B} and R_{B} are dual to one another. In particular L_{B}^{*}:V=\Hom(V,\Hom(V,F))\to \Hom(V,F) is the same as R_{B} and vice versa.

Proof: This is a matter of keeping track of definitions. If v\in V, then the associated linear map from \Hom(V,F) to F is evaluation: \delta_{v}:f\mapsto f(v). Then L_{B}^{*}(\delta_{v})=\delta_{v}\circ L_{B} But \delta_{v}\circ L_{B}(w) = \delta_{v}(w\mapsto B(w,\cdot))=B(w,v) which is the same as R_{B}(v)=B(\cdot, v) so R_{B}(v)(w)=B(w,v).

The Matrix of a Bilinear Form

Given a bilinear form on (finite dimensional) V, we can choose a basis E=\{e_{1},\ldots e_{n}\} for V. Then B is determined by the values B(e_{i},e_{j}) for 1\le i,j\le n. Let M be the n\times n matrix with these entries. Then it’s easy to see by linearity that B(v,w) = [v]_{E}^{\top}M[w]_{E}

Conversely, given an n\times n matrix, the formula above gives a bilinear form on F^{n}. Thus we may construct any bilinear form on V by choosing a basis (an isomorphism with F^{n}) and an n\times n matrix.

Example: Let V=F^{n} and choose integers p,q with p+q=n. Let M be the matrix

M = \left(\begin{matrix} I_{p} & 0 \\ 0 & -I_{q} \end{matrix}\right)

where I_{k} is the identity matrix. This creates a “pseudoEuclidean space” with signature p,q. If p=3 and q=1 we obtain Minkowski space, which is the flat spacetime of special relativity.

One can show (see Conrad, Theorem 2.5) that the matrix M defined above is the matrix of the linear map R_{B}:V\to \Hom(V,F) relative to the choice of the basis E on V and its dual on \Hom(V,F). He uses this to give a “coordinate free” proof of the following facts:

  1. B is symmetric iff M is symmetric.
  2. B is skew-symmetric (alternating) iff M^{\top}=-M^{\top}.

One can also show (either in a fancy way as Conrad does, or by calculating) that if C is a change of basis matrix from E_{1} to E_{2}, and B is a bilinear form on V with matrix M relative to E_{1}, then the matrix of B relative to E_{2} is M' = C^{\top}M C.

If B_{2} is a bilinear forms on V_{2}, and L:V_{1}\to V_{2} is a linear map, then B_{1}(v,w)=B_{2}(Lv,Lw) is a bilinear form on V_{1} (called the pullback of B_{2} to B_{1}).

If we are given forms B_{1} and B_{2} on V_{1} and B_{2} respectively, then we say B_{1} and B_{2} are equivalent if there is a linear isomorphism from V_{1} to V_{2} so that the pullback of B_{2} is B_{1}.

In matrix terms, two bilinear forms B_{1} and B_{2} with matrix representations M_{1} and M_{2} are equivalent if there is an invertible matrix C so that C^{\top}M_{1}C = M_{2}.

Bilinear Forms and Matrix Representations

Math 5250 — Week 12

Overview

  • A bilinear form generalizes an inner product, relaxing conditions such as symmetry or positive definiteness.
  • We consider only vector spaces and bilinear forms over \R or \C.
  • The theory over fields like \Q or finite fields is also interesting; see Keith Conrad’s article Bilinear Forms for the more general situation.

Reference: https://kconrad.math.uconn.edu/blurbs/linmultialg/bilinearform.pdf

Bilinear Forms

Definition

The definition doesn’t depend on the field F.

Definition. Let F be a field and V a vector space over F. A bilinear form on V is a function B:V\times V\to F that is linear in each variable when the other is fixed: \begin{aligned} B(au+v,w)&=aB(u,w)+B(v,w)\\ B(u,av+w)&=aB(u,v)+B(u,w) \end{aligned}

  • B is symmetric if B(v,w)=B(w,v) for all v,w\in V.
  • B is antisymmetric or skew-symmetric if B(v,w)=-B(w,v) for all v,w\in V.
  • B is alternating if B(v,v)=0 for all v.

Examples

  • An inner product over a real vector space is a symmetric bilinear form.
  • This isn’t true over a complex vector space, because we required such inner products to be hermitian.
  • If A\in M_n(F), then B(v,w)=v^{\top}Aw is a bilinear form.
  • If (u,v) and (a,b) are in F^2, then ub-va is a skew-symmetric bilinear form on F^2.

Alternating and Skew-Symmetric Forms

Lemma. Over \R or \C (or indeed any field of characteristic different from 2), alternating forms are the same thing as skew-symmetric forms.

Proof.

  • If B is alternating, then B(x+y,x+y)=0=B(x,y)+B(y,x), so B is skew-symmetric.
  • If B is skew-symmetric, then B(x,x)=-B(x,x), so 2B(x,x)=0, so B is alternating, assuming F does not have characteristic two.

From now on, every vector space is over \R or \C.

Symmetric and Skew-Symmetric Parts

Proposition. Every bilinear form B on V is expressible uniquely as a sum B(u,v)=B_1(u,v)+B_2(u,v) for all u,v\in V, where B_1 is symmetric and B_2 is skew-symmetric (alternating).

Proof. Let B_1(u,v)=\frac{B(u,v)+B(v,u)}{2},\qquad B_2(u,v)=\frac{B(u,v)-B(v,u)}{2}.

To see uniqueness, if B=B_1+B_2=B_1'+B_2', then B_1-B_1'=B_2'-B_2. The left side is symmetric, the right side alternating, so both sides are zero.

The Polarization Identity

Suppose B is symmetric. Then B(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\big(B(x+y,x+y)-B(x,x)-B(y,y)\big).

So a symmetric bilinear form is determined by its values B(v,v) for all v\in V.

Orthogonality

Orthogonality

  • w\perp v when B(w,v)=0.
  • For a subspace W\subset V, v\perp W provided B(v,w)=0 for all w\in W.

In general this relation need not be symmetric: one can have B(v,w)=0 (so v\perp w) but B(w,v)\neq 0, so w\not\perp v.

For symmetric or alternating forms the relation is symmetric, since B(v,w)=\pm B(w,v) for all v,w\in V.

One can show (Conrad, Theorem 1.17) that \perp is a symmetric relation if and only if B is either symmetric or alternating.

Orthogonal Complements

If B is symmetric or alternating and W\subset V is a subspace, define W^{\perp}=\{v\in V: v\perp w\quad\forall w\in W\}.

Nonzero vectors can be perpendicular to themselves, so one can have W\cap W^{\perp}\neq 0.

Example. Let B be the alternating form on F^2 B((x,y),(u,v))=xv-yu, and let W be the span of (1,1). Then W^{\perp}=W. In fact every v\in V is perpendicular to itself.

Restrictions and Orthogonal Direct Sums

  • If W\subset V is a subspace, the restriction of a bilinear form B on V is a bilinear form on W.
  • If V_1,V_2 have bilinear forms B_1,B_2, define B=B_1\oplus B_2 on V_1\oplus V_2 by B((x,y),(z,w))=B_1(x,z)+B_2(y,w).

V_1 and V_2 are orthogonal in the sum, so this is called the orthogonal direct sum of B_1 and B_2. The restriction of B to V_1 and V_2 recovers B_1 and B_2.

Bilinear Forms and Duality

The Maps L_B and R_B

Let V be a vector space over F with a bilinear form B.

  • For fixed v\in V, f(w)=B(v,w) is a linear functional on V, an element of \Hom(V,F). This gives a linear map L_B:V\to\Hom(V,F).
  • Alternatively, fix v\in V and define g_v(w)=B(w,v). This gives a map R_B:V\to\Hom(V,F).

L_B and R_B Are Dual

Proposition. If V is finite dimensional, and we identify V with \Hom(\Hom(V,F),F), then the two maps L_B and R_B are dual to one another. In particular \hat{L}_B:V=\Hom(\Hom(V,F),F)\to\Hom(V,F) is the same as R_B and vice versa.

Proof That L_B and R_B Are Dual

This is a matter of keeping track of definitions. If v\in V, the associated linear map from \Hom(V,F) to F is evaluation, \delta_v:f\mapsto f(v). Then \hat{L}_B(\delta_v)=\delta_v\circ L_B. But \hat{L}_B(\delta_v)(w)=(\delta_v\circ L_B)(w)=\delta_v(L_B(w))=L_B(w)(v)=B(w,v). Since R_B(v)=B(\cdot,v), we have R_B(v)(w)=B(w,v), so \hat{L}_B(\delta_v)=R_B(v).

The other duality follows similarly or via the fact that \hat{\hat{L}}_B=L_B=\hat{R}_B.

The Matrix of a Bilinear Form

The Matrix of a Bilinear Form

Choose a basis E=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\} for (finite dimensional) V. Then B is determined by the values B(e_i,e_j), 1\le i,j\le n. Let M be the n\times n matrix with these entries. By linearity, B(v,w)=[v]_E^{\top}M[w]_E.

Conversely, an n\times n matrix gives a bilinear form on F^n by this formula. So we may construct any bilinear form on V by choosing a basis (an isomorphism with F^n) and an n\times n matrix.

Example: Pseudo-Euclidean Space

Let V=F^n and choose integers p,q with p+q=n. Let M=\begin{pmatrix} I_p & 0\\ 0 & -I_q\end{pmatrix}, where I_k is the identity matrix.

  • This creates a pseudo-Euclidean space with signature p,q.
  • If p=3 and q=1 we obtain Minkowski space, the flat spacetime of special relativity.

The Matrix and R_B

One can show (Conrad, Theorem 2.5) that M is the matrix of the linear map R_B:V\to\Hom(V,F) relative to the basis E on V and its dual on \Hom(V,F).

Conrad uses this to give a “coordinate free” proof that:

  1. B is symmetric iff M is symmetric.
  2. B is skew-symmetric (alternating) iff M^{\top}=-M.

Change of Basis

If C is a change of basis matrix whose columns express the elements of a basis E_2 in terms of E_1, and B is a bilinear form on V with matrix M relative to E_1, then the matrix of B relative to E_2 is M'=C^{\top}MC.

This can be shown either in a fancy way, as Conrad does, or by calculating.

Pullbacks and Equivalence

If B_2 is a bilinear form on V_2 and L:V_1\to V_2 is a linear map, then B_1(v,w)=B_2(Lv,Lw) is a bilinear form on V_1, called the pullback of B_2 to V_1.

Given forms B_1 and B_2 on V_1 and V_2 respectively, B_1 and B_2 are equivalent if there is a linear isomorphism from V_1 to V_2 so that the pullback of B_2 is B_1.

In matrix terms, B_1 and B_2 with matrices M_1 and M_2 are equivalent if there is an invertible matrix C with C^{\top}M_1C=M_2.

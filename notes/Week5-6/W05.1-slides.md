# Inner Products over $\mathbb{R}$

## Inner Products

**Definition.** Let $V$ be a real vector space. An *inner product* on $V$ is a map $J:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}$ such that:

a. (symmetry) $J(u,v)=J(v,u)$ for all $u,v\in V$;
b. (linearity) $J(au_1+bu_2,v)=aJ(u_1,v)+bJ(u_2,v)$. By symmetry, $J$ is also linear in the second variable, so $J$ is *bilinear*;
c. (positive definiteness) $J(v,v)>0$ for all nonzero $v\in V$.

We often write $J(u,v)=\langle u,v\rangle$.

A real vector space together with an inner product is a (real) *inner product space*.

## Example: The Dot Product

**Example.** The Euclidean dot product on $\mathbb{R}^n$:
$$
J((a_1,\ldots,a_n),(b_1,\ldots,b_n))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_ib_i=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)\cdot(b_1,\ldots,b_n).
$$

Inner product spaces make sense in infinite dimensions too, but we focus on the finite-dimensional case.

## Geometry in an Inner Product Space

An inner product lets us measure the size of a vector and the distance between vectors:
$$
\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle},\qquad d(u,v)=\sqrt{\langle u-v,u-v\rangle}=\|u-v\|.
$$

This distance is a *metric*:

a. $d(u,v)\ge0$, and $d(u,v)=0$ if and only if $u=v$;
b. $d(u,w)\le d(u,v)+d(v,w)$ (the triangle inequality).

**Example.** For the standard dot product, this is the Pythagorean distance
$$
d(u,v)=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(u_i-v_i)^2},
$$
and $\{v:\|v\|=r\}$ is a sphere of radius $r$.

## The Matrix of an Inner Product

Let $E=\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$ be a basis for $V$. Define the $n\times n$ matrix $[J]^E$ by
$$
[J]^E_{ij}=\langle e_i,e_j\rangle.
$$
Then
$$
\Big\langle \sum a_ie_i,\sum b_ie_i\Big\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}a_1&\cdots&a_n\end{bmatrix}[J]^E\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\\vdots\\b_n\end{bmatrix},
$$
that is,
$$
\langle a,b\rangle=[a]_E^\top[J]^E[b]_E.
$$

**Definition.** If $X$ is $m\times n$, its *transpose* $X^\top$ is the $n\times m$ matrix with $X^\top_{ij}=X_{ji}$.

**Remark.** $(XY)^\top=Y^\top X^\top$.

## Properties of $[J]^E$

If $X=[J]^E$ comes from an inner product, then:

a. $X$ is *symmetric*;
b. $X$ has rank $n$;
c. $X$ is *positive definite*: $[a]_E^\top X[a]_E>0$ when $[a]_E\neq0$;
d. if $T$ is another basis and $[a]_T=[1]_E^T[a]_E$, then
$$
[J]^E=\big([1]_E^T\big)^\top[J]^T[1]_E^T.
$$

## Inner Products from Matrices

**Proposition.** If $X$ is a real symmetric positive definite $n\times n$ matrix, then
$$
J(a,b)=a^\top Xb
$$
is an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and $X=[J]^E$ for the standard basis $E$.

**Proposition.** If $J$ is an inner product on $V$ and $E$ is a basis, then $X=[J]^E$ is symmetric and positive definite, and
$$
J(a,b)=[a]_E^\top[J]^E[b]_E.
$$
In these coordinates, the surfaces $\{a:J(a,a)=r\}$ are ellipsoids.

# Orthogonality

## Orthogonality

Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional inner product space with inner product $J$.

**Definition.** Vectors $a,b\in V$ are *orthogonal* (relative to $J$) if $J(a,b)=0$.

**Example.** For the standard Euclidean inner product on $\mathbb{R}^n$, orthogonal means perpendicular.

## The Gram-Schmidt Process

**Proposition.** (Gram-Schmidt) Let $E=\{e_1,\ldots,e_m\}$ be a linearly independent subset of $V$ spanning $W$. Then there are vectors $U=\{u_1,\ldots,u_m\}$ such that:

1. $U$ is an *orthonormal set*:
    a. $J(u_i,u_j)=0$ if $i\neq j$;
    b. $\|u_i\|=1$ for $i=1,\ldots,m$;
2. for $j=1,\ldots,m$, the span $W_j$ of $U_j=\{u_1,\ldots,u_j\}$ equals the span of $E_j=\{e_1,\ldots,e_j\}$.

## Consequences of Gram-Schmidt

Part (2) says the change of basis is triangular:
$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1&=a_{11}e_1\\
u_2&=a_{12}e_1+a_{22}e_2\\
&\;\;\vdots\\
u_j&=a_{1j}e_1+\cdots+a_{jj}e_j
\end{aligned}
$$

If $E$ spans $V$, then $[J]^U$ is the identity matrix: in the $U$ basis, $J$ is the standard dot product,
$$
J(a,b)=[a]_U\cdot[b]_U=\sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i,\qquad a=\sum a_iu_i,\ b=\sum b_iu_i.
$$

## Proof of Gram-Schmidt

We proceed by induction. Let
$$
u_1=\frac{e_1}{\|e_1\|}.
$$
This handles the case where $W$ is one-dimensional.

Suppose $u_1,\ldots,u_j$ are orthonormal, their span equals the span of $E_j$, and the change of basis is triangular. Let
$$
v_{j+1}=e_{j+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{j}J(e_{j+1},u_i)u_i.
$$

## Proof of Gram-Schmidt (continued)

For $k=1,\ldots,j$:
$$
J(v_{j+1},u_k)=J(e_{j+1},u_k)-\sum_{i=1}^{j}J(e_{j+1},u_i)J(u_i,u_k).
$$
Since $J(u_i,u_k)=0$ unless $i=k$, where it is $1$, this is zero.

Also, the span of $\{u_1,\ldots,u_j,v_{j+1}\}$ equals the span of $\{e_1,\ldots,e_{j+1}\}$.

Let
$$
u_{j+1}=\frac{v_{j+1}}{\|v_{j+1}\|}.
$$
This constructs $U$ as desired, and the result follows by induction. $\square$

## Expansion in an Orthonormal Basis

**Proposition.** If $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ is an orthonormal basis for $V$, then for every $v\in V$,
$$
v=\sum_{i=1}^n J(v,u_i)u_i.
$$

# Projection

## Orthogonal Complements

Let $W\subset V$ be a subspace.

**Definition.** The *orthogonal complement* of $W$ is
$$
W^\perp=\{v\in V: J(v,w)=0\text{ for all }w\in W\}.
$$

**Proposition.**

a. $W^\perp$ is a subspace of $V$.
b. The map $W\oplus W^\perp\to V$, $(x,y)\mapsto x+y$, is an isomorphism. In particular, $\dim W+\dim W^\perp=n$.
c. $(W^\perp)^\perp=W$.

## Orthogonal Projection

**Proposition.** Inverting $W\oplus W^\perp\to V$ gives a map $P_W:V\to V$ with image $W$ and kernel $W^\perp$, the *orthogonal projection* onto $W$. Similarly, $P_{W^\perp}$ has image $W^\perp$ and kernel $W$. Moreover:

- $P_WP_W=P_W$ and $P_{W^\perp}P_{W^\perp}=P_{W^\perp}$ (they are *idempotent*);
- $P_W+P_{W^\perp}$ is the identity.

In an orthonormal basis $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ with $u_1,\ldots,u_m$ spanning $W$:
$$
[P_W]=\begin{pmatrix}I_{m\times m}&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
[P_{W^\perp}]=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&I_{(n-m)\times(n-m)}\end{pmatrix}.
$$

# Closest Points

## Distance to a Subspace

The *distance* from $v\in V$ to $W$ is the minimum of $\|v-w\|$ as $w$ ranges over $W$.

**Proposition.** The distance from $v$ to $W$ is
$$
\|v-P_W(v)\|=\|P_{W^\perp}(v)\|.
$$

## The One-Dimensional Case

If $W$ is spanned by $w$ and $v\notin W$, then
$$
\|v-aw\|^2=J(v,v)-2aJ(v,w)+a^2J(w,w)
$$
is minimized at $a=\dfrac{J(v,w)}{J(w,w)}$. The vector from the closest point to $v$ is
$$
v_0=v-\frac{J(v,w)}{J(w,w)}w=P_{W^\perp}(v),
$$
and
$$
\|v_0\|^2=J(v,v)-\frac{J(v,w)^2}{J(w,w)}>0.
$$

## The Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

**Proposition.** (Cauchy-Schwarz) For $v,w$ in a real inner product space,
$$
J(v,w)^2\le J(v,v)J(w,w),
$$
or equivalently
$$
|J(v,w)|\le\sqrt{J(v,v)J(w,w)},
$$
with equality if and only if $v$ is a multiple of $w$.

# Application: Ordinary Least Squares

## The Least Squares Problem

Given measurements $(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ik})_{i=1}^N$ and $(y_i)_{i=1}^N$, we suspect a linear relation
$$
y=\sum_{j=1}^k m_jx_j+b
$$
up to random error.

The *least squares* choice of $m_j$ and $b$ minimizes
$$
E(m,b)=\sum_{i=1}^N\Big(y_i-\sum_{j=1}^k m_jx_{ij}-b\Big)^2.
$$

## Least Squares as Projection

Let $Y\in\mathbb{R}^N$ have entries $y_i$, let $X$ be the $N\times(k+1)$ matrix with $i^{th}$ row $(x_{i1},\ldots,x_{ik},1)$, and let $M=(m_1,\ldots,m_k,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{k+1}$. Then
$$
E(m,b)=\|Y-XM\|^2.
$$

As $M$ varies, $XM$ ranges over the column space of $X$, so $E$ is minimized when $Y-XM$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$:
$$
X^\top(Y-XM)=0.
$$

## The Normal Equations

Let $D=X^\top X$, a $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ matrix. If $X$ has rank $k+1$, then $D$ is invertible and
$$
M=D^{-1}X^\top Y.
$$

The closest point in the column space of $X$ is
$$
XM=XD^{-1}X^\top Y,
$$
the projection of $Y$ onto the column space of $X$.

## Projection Operator in General

Choose a basis $E$ for $V$ and write $J$ for $[J]^E$. Let $X$ be the matrix with columns $[w_1]_E,\ldots,[w_k]_E$, where $w_1,\ldots,w_k$ is a basis for $W$.

For $Y\in V$, we want $Y-XM$ orthogonal to the columns of $X$:
$$
X^\top J(Y-XM)=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M=(X^\top JX)^{-1}X^\top JY.
$$

So the matrix of $P_W$ is
$$
[P_W]_E=X(X^\top JX)^{-1}X^\top J,
$$
and one checks directly that $[P_W][P_W]=[P_W]$.

# Complex (Hermitian) Inner Products

## Hermitian Inner Products

**Definition.** Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex vector space. A *Hermitian* inner product on $V$ is a function $J:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}$ that:

1. is linear in the first variable and conjugate linear in the second:
$$
J(au+bv,w)=aJ(u,w)+bJ(v,w),\qquad J(u,av+bw)=\overline{a}J(u,v)+\overline{b}J(u,w);
$$
2. is conjugate symmetric: $J(u,v)=\overline{J(v,u)}$ (so $J(u,u)$ is real);
3. is positive definite: $J(u,u)\ge0$, with $J(u,u)=0$ only if $u=0$.

As in the real case, $\|v\|=\sqrt{J(v,v)}$.

## The Matrix of a Hermitian Inner Product

Let $E=\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ be a basis and set $[J]^E_{ij}=J(v_i,v_j)$. Since
$$
J\Big(\sum a_iv_i,\sum b_jv_j\Big)=\sum a_i\overline{b_j}J(v_i,v_j),
$$
we have
$$
J(u,v)=[u]_E^\top[J]^E\,\overline{[v]_E}.
$$

The matrix $[J]^E$ is *Hermitian*: its transpose is its conjugate,
$$
([J]^E)^\top=\overline{[J]^E}.
$$

## Gram-Schmidt for Hermitian Inner Products

If $\{v_1,\ldots,v_n\}$ is a basis for a Hermitian inner product space, let
$$
u_1=\frac{v_1}{\|v_1\|},
$$
and, assuming $u_1,\ldots,u_k$ are orthonormal,
$$
u_{k+1}'=v_{k+1}-\sum_{i=1}^k J(v_{k+1},u_i)u_i,\qquad u_{k+1}=\frac{u_{k+1}'}{\|u_{k+1}'\|}.
$$

This gives an orthonormal basis $u_1,\ldots,u_n$ for $V$ in which the span of $u_1,\ldots,u_j$ equals the span of $v_1,\ldots,v_j$.
